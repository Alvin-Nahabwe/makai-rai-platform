## How I checked

I re-ran the method cells read-only on a copy of the cached Adult file (SHA-256 matches the pin) in `scratchpad/nbreview-scratch-C-10/`, with the project venv and no network. I recomputed k and l, then recomputed them again with the income labels normalised. I ran `claimMatches` from `lib/claims/pattern.ts` over every source cell and every output of the executed copy.

The main finding: Adult's `income` column holds **four** values, not two: `<=50K` (24,720), `<=50K.` (12,435), `>50K` (7,841) and `>50K.` (3,846). The test rows carry a trailing full stop. `l_diversity` counts distinct values, so a group in which everyone has income at most 50K, split between `<=50K` and `<=50K.`, scores l = 2 when the true l is 1. In the executed run, 30 of the 181 groups left after "then to l >= 2" (664 rows) hold only one real income value. The reported l = 2, and the residual-risk sentence "every such group holds at least 2 different income value(s)", are false for this data. With the labels normalised, the same code removes 958 rows (2.0%), not 294, leaves 151 groups and reaches a true l = 2. The Female and Male averages then change by 0.159 and 0.121, not 0.011 and 0.025.

### 3. "What this does not show" is honest: **change**

Cell 23 is honest in most of what it says. Two points are not:
- After the income fix, a binary sensitive field with l = 2 still gives the value away in many groups: 41 of the 151 groups (15,490 rows) have one income value for 90% or more of their members, and one group has 99.9%. The notebook teaches l = 2 as the goal and never says that l counts values without regard to how they are spread.
- "They do not cover ... someone who already knows the person is in the data" has the threat model backwards. k-anonymity assumes exactly that attacker. What k and l miss is membership disclosure, and background knowledge beyond the quasi-identifiers.

- **Cell 23** (`## What this does not show, and where to go next`). Replace the bullet
  `- **k and l are one lens.** They do not cover attribute inference by a model, membership tests, or someone who already knows the person is in the data.`
  with
  `- **k and l are one lens.** They do not cover attribute inference by a model, tests of whether a person is in the data at all, or what someone already knows about a person beyond the quasi-identifiers, which can rule out values in that person's group.`
  `- **l = 2 can still give the value away.** l counts how many different sensitive values a group has, not how they are spread. In a group of 1,000 people where 999 have one income value and one has the other, l is 2, yet knowing that a person is in the group tells you their income almost surely. Some groups in this example are like that. Look at the share of the commonest value in each group before you rely on l.`
