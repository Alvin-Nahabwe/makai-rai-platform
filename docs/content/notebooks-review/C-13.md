## Items

### C1-a: change. Cell 10 ("### Step 3: one number per metric for the whole comparison"), the "What it does not show" paragraph

Criteria 1, 3 and 7. Two problems.

1. The impossibility statement is wrong. If true and false positive rates are equal across groups, a group's selection rate is FPR + p × (TPR − FPR), where p is its real rate of the favourable outcome. With different p, equal selection rates and equal error rates together need TPR = FPR: predictions that carry no information about the outcome. The notebook's condition "the model is not perfect" is the wrong one. A perfect model (TPR 1, FPR 0) has selection rate p, so it fails demographic parity too. That condition belongs to the different calibration-versus-error-rate conflict. Also, "they cannot all be zero at once" is false for a model that labels everyone the same.
2. The size rule does not protect the error-rate gaps. `MIN_GROUP_SIZE` counts everyone in a group. The true positive rate uses only the people who really have the favourable outcome. In this run, "Other" (113 people) is kept but holds 11 such people, and its true positive rate of 4 in 11 (0.364) alone sets the race equal opportunity and equalised odds gap of 0.344. The step's reason for the size rule ("a group of ten people can produce an extreme rate by chance and then it alone decides the gap") is happening here, and the text implies it has been prevented.

Replace:

> **What it does not show.** Which definition is the right one. They measure different things, and unless the groups have the same real rate of the favourable outcome they cannot all be zero at once (when the groups differ in real rates and the model is not perfect, equal selection rates and equal error rates cannot both hold). Picking one is the "Stop and decide" step.

with:

> **What it does not show.** Which definition is the right one. They measure different things, and when the groups have different real rates of the favourable outcome they cannot all be zero at once: equal selection rates and equal error rates can then hold together only for a model whose predictions carry no information about the true outcome, such as one that labels everyone the same. Picking one is the "Stop and decide" step.
>
> It also does not make every gap trustworthy. The size rule counts everyone in a group, but the true positive rate uses only the group's people who really have the favourable outcome, and the false positive rate only those who do not. A group can meet the size rule and still hold only a handful of people who really have the favourable outcome, and then its true positive rate, and any gap it sets, can swing by chance. Read the true-positive-rate gaps beside their intervals in Step 5.

### C1-b: change. Cell 14 ("### Step 5: how sure can we be? Group sizes and bootstrap intervals"), the "What it shows" paragraph

Criteria 1 and 7. Each gap is `MetricFrame.difference()`, which by Fairlearn's definition is `group_max() - group_min()`. It is never below zero, so a bootstrap interval of it can never "include zero" (in this run every interval's low end is above zero, for example 0.018 for the sex true-positive-rate gap). The max-minus-min also comes out above zero by chance alone: with race labels shuffled, the race true-positive-rate gap has median 0.138 and 97.5th percentile 0.310. As written, the reading rule cannot be applied, and a team would take every interval as evidence of a real gap.

Replace:

> **What it shows.** How much of a gap could be luck. A wide interval that includes zero means the data cannot tell a real gap from noise. A narrow interval far from zero means the gap is likely to show up again on similar data.

with:

> **What it shows.** How much of a gap could be luck. Each gap here is the highest group value minus the lowest, so it is never below zero, and chance alone pushes it above zero, more so when groups are small or there are many of them. A narrow interval well above zero means the gap is likely to show up again on similar data.

### C7-a: change. Cell 12 ("### Step 4: calibration within each group"), the last sentence of "What this does"

Criterion 7. scikit-learn defines the Brier score as "the mean squared difference between the predicted probability and the actual outcome". That is not a calibration measure on its own. It also moves with how well the score separates the two outcomes and with the group's base rate. In this run, women get 0.053 and men 0.127, but the calibration part of each is about the same (0.0004 against 0.0003). The gap comes from the base rates (0.107 against 0.305). As written, a team would read it as "the model is better calibrated for women".

Replace:

> The Brier score summarises the same idea in one number per group (lower is better).

with:

> The Brier score is the average squared gap between each person's score and their real outcome (0 or 1), one number per group, and lower is better. It is not a calibration number on its own: it also changes with how well the score separates the two outcomes and with how common the favourable outcome is in the group, so a group where the favourable outcome is rare tends to get a lower Brier score even when its curve is no closer to the diagonal. Compare groups on the curves, and read the Brier score only beside them.
