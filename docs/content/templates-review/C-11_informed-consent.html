C-11: Informed Consent Framework

Procedural Control in the MAK-AI Responsible AI Toolkit

Grounding: Draws on the information requirements of GDPR Articles 13 and 14, as one example of a data protection law. It also draws on the WHO guidance Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health (2021) and on dynamic consent models. This template addresses AI-specific consent challenges that standard research ethics forms do not cover.

1. Purpose & Data Practices

Say clearly what data you collect, why, and how it will be used. Use plain language that non-experts can follow.

For example, Uganda's National Guidelines for Research Involving Humans as Research Participants (UNCST, 2025) cover AI in section 16.6. They list consent to use data, algorithmic fairness, data privacy and data ownership among the issues an AI protocol must address. They also require data-protection clearance, from the national body for information systems and data protection, before ethics submission. Name the steps that apply to you.

2. AI-Specific Disclosures

These disclosures go beyond standard consent forms to address AI/ML-specific concerns. Participants should understand how AI will use their data.

Important: If the data will train models that make decisions about individuals (for example credit scoring or clinical diagnosis), say so clearly. Participants should be able to contest such decisions, because otherwise they cannot correct an error that affects them.

3. Participant Rights

List the rights your participants have under the law that applies to you (for example Uganda's Data Protection and Privacy Act 2019) and under your ethics approval.

Rights to check against your law and ethics approval (tick only those that apply to your participants)

4. Consent Verification

Consent should be meaningful, not only a signature on a form, because a signature does not show that the person understood.

Local context: Consider literacy barriers (support oral consent protocols), power imbalances (especially in healthcare/humanitarian contexts), community consent models, and language accessibility (provide consent materials in local languages).

5. Dynamic Consent & Updates

Consent works best as an ongoing process rather than a one-time event, because purposes and uses can change.
